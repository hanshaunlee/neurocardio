01 · The problem

The problem with always-on AI

Cardiovascular disease kills roughly 17 million people every year — more than cancer, more than respiratory disease, more than anything else. A significant fraction of those deaths are from conditions that are both detectable and treatable, but only if caught in time.

The catch is that the most dangerous arrhythmias are intermittent. A patient can walk into a clinic feeling fine, get a clean ECG, and then suffer a fatal event three days later. The gold standard for catching these is continuous ambulatory monitoring: a small device the patient wears for 24 to 72 hours — or longer — that records and analyzes the heart non-stop.

Today's AI-powered ECG analysis works beautifully in the hospital. It doesn't work on a wearable. The problem is energy. A deep convolutional network running on a battery-powered device would drain it in hours. Wearable cardiac monitors instead use simple threshold detectors — they're efficient, but they miss the subtle patterns that modern deep learning catches.

There's a third option. It's been promising for years but rarely proven on real clinical data.

02 · The insight

Neurons don't run all the time

Your brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons, each one a tiny electrical device. But at any given moment, most of them are silent. A neuron only fires — only consumes energy — when the input to its cell body accumulates past a threshold. Then it fires a spike, resets, and goes quiet again. The whole system is exquisitely event-driven.

This is exactly the opposite of how a traditional neural network works. A CNN processes every pixel, every sample, every time step, regardless of whether anything interesting is happening. That's fine when you have a power outlet. It's fatal when you're running on a CR2032 coin cell.

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are artificial networks built around the same principle. Hidden units are leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons: they accumulate input, and only fire — only do work — when the accumulated charge crosses a threshold. In between firings, they're idle. On purpose-built neuromorphic chips like Intel's Loihi, each spike triggers a single cheap accumulate operation (~0.1 picojoules). A standard GPU multiply-accumulate costs ~3.7 pJ — 37× more expensive per operation, before even counting how many fewer operations an SNN performs.

03 · The data

The experiment

The claim sounds good. But does it hold up on a real clinical dataset, against real baselines, with a proper held-out test set? That's what this project tests.

The dataset is PTB-XL, the largest open 12-lead ECG database in the world: 21,837 recordings, each 10 seconds long, sampled at 100 Hz, annotated by two cardiologists. Each recording is labeled with one or more of five diagnostic superclasses defined by the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (AAMI):

NORM — Normal
No pathology found. Normal sinus rhythm.
7,596 positive in training set
MI — Myocardial Infarction
Heart attack. Death of cardiac muscle from a blocked artery. Often shows as ST-elevation or Q-waves.
4,379 positive in training set
STTC — ST/T-wave Change
Altered repolarization pattern. Can indicate ischemia, drug effects, or electrolyte imbalance.
4,186 positive in training set
CD — Conduction Disturbance
Abnormal electrical pathway. Bundle-branch blocks, AV blocks, or pacing abnormalities.
3,907 positive in training set
HYP — Hypertrophy
Thickened chamber wall, often from chronic hypertension. The hardest class — fewest examples, subtlest signal.
2,119 positive — rarest class, biggest challenge

A recording can carry multiple labels simultaneously — a patient can have both a conduction disturbance and signs of past infarction. This is a multi-label classification problem, which is harder than single-label and much closer to clinical reality.

We use the PTB-XL recommended stratified split: folds 1–8 for training (17,084 recordings), fold 9 for validation (2,146), fold 10 for the final test (2,158). Patients never appear in more than one fold — there is no data leakage. All three models trained on exactly the same data, evaluated on exactly the same test set.

04 · The network

The spiking network: S-TCN

The model is a Spiking Temporal Convolutional Network (S-TCN) — a deep network built entirely from LIF neurons. It processes the ECG in five stages:

1
Delta-modulation encoding
The raw voltage waveform is never fed directly to the network. Instead, a threshold encoder fires an ON spike when voltage rises by θ=0.15 mV, and an OFF spike when it falls by the same. Between crossings: silence, zero events, zero energy. Flat baseline = idle. The QRS complex (the big heartbeat spike) produces a burst of events. 12 leads × 2 directions = 24 event channels.
2
Six dilated spiking blocks
Six temporal convolutional blocks with exponentially increasing dilation (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32). Each block's receptive field doubles, so by block 6 every neuron can "see" the entire 10-second recording. Novel: membrane-gated residuals. Standard residual connections don't work in SNNs — adding binary spikes to binary spikes produces multi-bit activations. Instead, skip paths inject current directly into the output LIF's membrane potential. Spikes stay binary. A learnable gate per channel controls the balance.
3
Spiking attention pooling
A small LIF "gate" neuron accumulates per-step evidence and fires at the time-steps where the ECG is most informative (typically the QRS complex and the T-wave). Novel: this is the first event-driven attention mechanism for ECG SNNs. No softmax, no real-valued mixing — just a spike pattern that selects which moments matter. The classifier pools only from those time-steps.
4
Non-leaky integrator → diagnosis
A final dense LIF layer feeds a non-leaky integrator that accumulates evidence over Tdense extra steps. The membrane potential at the end of accumulation — one scalar per disease class — is passed through a sigmoid. Trained with binary cross-entropy.

06 · The results

The findings

We trained three models on the same data with the same training budget: the SNN (S-TCN), a parameter-matched 1-D CNN, and a parameter-matched 1-D ResNet. Then we evaluated all three on the held-out fold 10 — 2,158 recordings the models had never seen.

The metric is macro-AUROC (area under the ROC curve, averaged across all five disease classes). A score of 1.0 means perfect classification. A score of 0.5 means the model is no better than flipping a coin. Published state-of-the-art models on PTB-XL typically score between 0.87 and 0.93.

07 · The energy

Now look at the energy

The energy calculation uses the Horowitz (ISSCC 2014) constants, the standard reference in the neuromorphic hardware literature. A dense 32-bit floating-point multiply-accumulate on a 45 nm ASIC costs approximately 3.7 picojoules. A spike-triggered accumulate on a neuromorphic chip like Intel's Loihi costs approximately 0.1 pJ.

The SNN performs 40.6 million effective synaptic ops per inference — "effective" meaning we measured the actual per-layer spike rates on the validation set and multiplied. At 0.1 pJ/SOP that's 4.06 µJ per ECG window. The CNN performs 294.6 million MACs at 3.7 pJ each — 1,090 µJ. That's a factor of 287×, driven by two compounding effects: fewer total ops, and cheaper ops per unit.

Energy per inference

SNN energy assumes neuromorphic hardware (Loihi). CNN and ResNet assume dense ASIC. The 287× gap comes from both fewer ops (7.8×) and cheaper ops per unit (37×).

What does 287× actually mean?

Here's a concrete scenario: a wearable Holter monitor analyzing a 10-second ECG window every 10 seconds — 8,640 inferences over a 24-hour monitoring session.

The 3.6 pp AUROC gap is real. Whether it matters depends entirely on the deployment. For a wearable screening device — one that flags suspected pathology and routes the patient to a clinical follow-up ECG — the lower sensitivity is acceptable. You're trading a small increase in false negatives for a device that works for days on a single charge.

For definitive diagnosis in a clinical setting where you have a GPU and a power outlet, the CNN is the right choice.

One more thing to note about latency: the measured inference times here (1,500 ms for the SNN vs. 2 ms for the CNN) are an artifact of simulating spiking dynamics on a dense GPU in PyTorch. On actual Loihi 2 silicon, SNN inference is typically sub-millisecond. The latency comparison is not representative of deployed neuromorphic hardware.

08 · The training

Did the SNN actually converge?

A common concern with spiking networks is training instability. Surrogate-gradient backprop (the technique that makes SNNs trainable at all) is known to be sensitive to hyperparameters, and some architectures fail to learn meaningful representations.

The training curve below shows 200 full epochs of S-TCN on PTB-XL. It does learn. It converges cleanly to a validation AUROC of 0.8748 by epoch 155, and the best checkpoint holds through epoch 200 — no collapse, no divergence. The per-class lines show each disease superclass tracking upward together.
